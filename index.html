02 - Flagship Research

Predictive Geospatial Modelling of African Urban Crime Escape Routes in a Satellite Town

My research project focuses on the predictive geospatial modelling of post-crime escape routes, targeting street robberies in the South African town of Stellenbosch. While traditional models successfully predict where crimes occur, this study addresses the theoretical gap of forecasting the "journey-from-crime," or how offenders flee the scene. To achieve this, I am developing a Crime Risk Model to predict victimization likelihood and an Escape Terrain Model that evaluates potential escape routes based on localized environmental risks. The study utilizes deterministic routing algorithms and stochastic Monte Carlo simulations to model the escape paths of three distinct offender archetypes across these risk surfaces. Ultimately, aggregating these simulated paths will identify critical environmental chokepoints, providing law enforcement with actionable insights to optimize rapid response and strategic infrastructure placement.

Project Objectives +
  1. Conduct a literature review to determine the most appropriate South African environmental risk weightings and spatial routing algorithms for modelling post-robbery escape routes.
  2. Collect and prepare spatial data of Stellenbosch's environmental features alongside historical SAPS street robbery records to serve as the foundation for the models.
  3. Collect, clean and prepare infrastructure and land use types in Stellenbosch.
  4. Create an RTM of the risk of the street-robbery victimisation.
  5. Create an RTM cost raster of the likelihood of a fleeing offender choosing a particular street/cell for their escape.
  6. Simulate the escape paths of three different offender archetypes using routing algorithms to pinpoint critical environmental chokepoints.
  7. Assess the accuracy and predictive power of the first RTM using historical street robbery data supplied by SAPS.
Academic & Theoretical Interests +

Crime Pattern Theory (CPT): Offenders do not move randomly; their paths are shaped by familiar nodes, paths, and edges within their awareness space.

Geospatial Modeling for Urban Safety: Geospatial modeling applied to urban safety directly drives my development of predictive Crime Risk and Escape Terrain Models

Data Analysis: My proficiency in the R programming language supports advanced data science tasks, including the execution of stochastic Monte Carlo simulations and the aggregation of probability density surfaces.

Monte Carlo Stochastic Modelling: Introduces probabilistic variance into path prediction, capturing the uncertainty inherent in human decision-making under stress.

Chokepoint Prediction: Network analysis identifies topological bottlenecks where escape route diversity collapses-optimal locations for interception.

Offender Archetypes & Algorithmic Strategies investigated +

The simulation engine models three distinct offender utility functions:

Stealth-Oriented

Minimizes visibility and encounter probability. Pathfinding via least-cost path analysis through low-risk terrain corridors.

Speed-Oriented

Prioritizes temporal minimization. Routes computed using Dijkstra's algorithm on road network graphs with velocity-weighted edges.

Balanced-Utility

Optimizes a weighted composite of speed and stealth. Behaviour modelled through Stochastic / Monte Carlo simulation with dynamic risk-reward thresholds.

Methodology, Data & Expected Impact +

The workflow integrates high-resolution satellite imagery, OpenStreetMap network data, and cadastral layers within ArcGIS Pro and custom Python pipelines. RTM grids are calibrated using historical crime incident data, while escape routes are simulated across 100+ Monte Carlo iterations per archetype. Results are validated against known case trajectories to refine model sensitivity. The ultimate goal is a deployable decision-support tool for tactical policing and proactive urban planning in rapidly urbanizing African contexts.

07 - Academic Reflection

Learning Journey

Before starting this research project, I could confidently perform basic spatial analysis and use Geographic Information Systems (GIS) for general mapping tasks. I relied a lot on traditional, reactive methods like hotspot mapping to understand spatial patterns. My programming skills were solid, but I had limited experience with dynamic, predictive routing over complex cost surfaces.

Now, I can create and implement complex, predictive geospatial models that reflect dynamic human behavior. I have greatly improved my technical and conceptual skills by creating Risk Terrain Models (RTM) and using deterministic spatial routing algorithms, including a raster-based modified Dijkstra's algorithm and stochastic Monte Carlo simulations. I can effectively turn theoretical criminological ideas into quantitative spatial data, building hybrid impedance surfaces that balance an offender's priorities of risk aversion and speed to predict their escape routes after a crime.

This is evident in my successful development of a dual-model approach for Stellenbosch: the Crime Risk Model (CRM) and the new Escape Terrain Model (ETM). My methodological growth is clear in my ability to combine literature-based, localized environmental weights with algorithmic cost-surface analysis to find predictive spatial chokepoints for different offender types.

I still need to improve my strategies for managing the uncertainties related to spatial data, especially the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP) and the effects of administering data anonymization. Going forward, my next challenge will be to refine these predictive models by integrating more extensive empirical, micro-level validation data to minimize random noise and better consider how people make decisions under stress.
